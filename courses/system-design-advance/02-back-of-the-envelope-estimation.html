Back-of-the-Envelope Estimation

🧮Start Introduction: why we estimate

Hinglish samajh: Back-of-the-envelope estimation matlab kisi system ka size jaldi aur mote taur par nikalna: kitne servers, kitna storage, kitni bandwidth. Jeff Dean (Google) kehte hain ki isse hum pehle hi pata laga lete hain ki design kaam karega ya nahi. Interview mein exact number nahi chahiye; interviewer dekhta hai ki aap assumptions likhte ho, problem ko chhote steps mein todte ho, numbers round karte ho, units likhte ho, aur ek samajhdaar number tak pahunchte ho. Hamesha assumptions pehle likhein.

Imagine you are asked to design a service, and before you draw a single box, the interviewer asks: "How many servers will you need? How much storage? How much network bandwidth?" You cannot know the exact answer, because the product does not exist yet. What you can do is make a quick, rough calculation using a few sensible assumptions. This skill is called back-of-the-envelope estimation, because the calculation is small enough to fit on the back of an envelope.

Jeff Dean, a Google Senior Fellow, describes it as using thought experiments and common performance numbers to get a good feel for which designs will meet the requirements. In other words, estimation helps you check whether an idea is even possible, before you spend months building it.

In an interview, nobody expects an exact number. The interviewer wants to see how you think: do you state your assumptions, do you break a big problem into small steps, do you round numbers sensibly, do you label units, and do you finish with a number that makes sense? A rough answer with a clear process beats a precise answer you cannot explain.

This chapter gives you the tools in three groups: the numbers you must remember (powers of two, latency numbers, availability numbers), a complete worked example (Twitter-like QPS and storage), and a set of habits (rounding, assumptions, units) that make your estimates trustworthy.

✨ Features

  • It checks quickly whether a design can work at all.
  • It shows your thinking process, which is what interviewers grade.
  • It uses a small set of numbers that you can memorize.

✅ Benefits

  • Avoids building a system that is far too small or far too expensive.
  • Helps choose between designs (for example, whether one database is enough).
  • Gives you confidence and structure when the problem feels huge.

⚠️ Problems

  • Wrong assumptions give wrong answers, so state them clearly and be ready to change them.
  • Chasing exact numbers wastes time. Stay approximate.
  • Forgetting units (MB vs GB, per day vs per second) is the most common mistake.

Interview questions and answers

Why do we do back-of-the-envelope estimation in a design interview?
To check whether a design is feasible and to size it: traffic, storage, bandwidth, cache and servers. It also shows the interviewer that you think with numbers, not only with boxes and arrows.
How accurate should the answer be?
Only roughly right. Being within the correct order of magnitude (for example about 55 PB, not 5 PB or 550 PB) is what matters. The process and the stated assumptions matter more than the final digit.
Cross question: What if the interviewer disagrees with an assumption?
Good, change it and recalculate. That is why you write assumptions down: you can swap one number and quickly produce the new result.
What should you estimate first?
Start with users and usage, then QPS, then storage. Those two answers drive most other decisions (database choice, sharding, caching, servers).

🔢Step 1 Power of two and data sizes

Hinglish samajh: Computer data ko powers of two mein ginta hai, par estimation mein hum har step ko lagbhag 1000 guna maan lete hain. 1 byte ek letter ke barabar, 1 KB lagbhag hazaar bytes, 1 MB lagbhag das lakh, 1 GB lagbhag ek arab, 1 TB lagbhag ek kharab bytes, aur 1 PB uske hazaar guna. Upar jaane ke liye 1000 se bhaag, neeche jaane ke liye 1000 se guna. Hamesha units likhein aur bit aur byte ka fark yaad rakhein (1 byte = 8 bits).

Computers count in powers of two, so data sizes are built from them. The key idea is simple: every time you climb one step in the size ladder, the number grows by about a thousand. One byte is the basic unit (it holds about one English character). A kilobyte (KB) is about a thousand bytes, a megabyte (MB) is about a million bytes, a gigabyte (GB) is about a billion bytes, a terabyte (TB) is about a trillion bytes, and a petabyte (PB) is about a quadrillion bytes.

The exact values come from powers of two: 2^10 is 1,024, which is close to a thousand; 2^20 is 1,048,576, close to a million; 2^30 is 1,073,741,824, close to a billion; and so on. In estimation we ignore the small difference and treat each step as exactly one thousand. This keeps the mental math easy and the answers close enough.

Why does this matter? When you calculate storage, the result is a long number of bytes, and you must convert it to something readable. For example, 30,000,000 MB means nothing at a glance, but "30 TB" is meaningful. Knowing the ladder lets you convert quickly: divide by a thousand to go up one unit, multiply by a thousand to go down.

It also helps to remember the rough size of common things. A character is 1 byte in ASCII (more for other scripts). A typical integer is 4 bytes, and a long integer is 8 bytes. A UUID is 16 bytes in binary. A short text message is a few hundred bytes, a photo is a few hundred kilobytes to a few megabytes, and a minute of video is tens of megabytes. These are assumptions you state out loud, not facts to memorize exactly.

Interview questions and answers

How many bytes are in a KB, MB, GB, TB and PB?
About a thousand, a million, a billion, a trillion and a quadrillion. Exactly they are 2^10, 2^20, 2^30, 2^40 and 2^50 bytes.
Why use approximations like "1 KB = 1,000 bytes"?
The error is only about 2% to 12% and the math becomes easy to do in your head, which is what an interview needs.
Cross question: What is the difference between a byte and a bit?
A byte is 8 bits. Network speeds are often quoted in bits per second, while storage is quoted in bytes, so divide or multiply by 8 when you convert.
Quick check: 10 million photos of 2 MB each, how much storage?
10,000,000 x 2 MB = 20,000,000 MB = 20 TB.

⏱️Step 2 Latency numbers every programmer should know

Hinglish samajh: Latency matlab kisi kaam mein lagne wala samay. CPU cache sabse tez (lagbhag 0.5 ns), phir RAM (100 ns), phir SSD (150 microsecond), phir hard disk seek (10 ms), aur doosre region ke data center tak jaana (150 ms). Agar 1 nanosecond ko 1 second maan lein, to RAM 100 second, SSD lagbhag 1.7 din aur region-to-region lagbhag 5 saal ka hoga. Seekh: memory tez hai, disk dheemi hai, disk seeks se bacho, aur network par bhejne se pehle data compress karo.

Latency is the time an operation takes. Some operations are extremely fast, and some are painfully slow, and the difference between them is enormous. Reading from the CPU cache takes about half a nanosecond. Reading from main memory takes about 100 nanoseconds. Reading randomly from an SSD takes about 150 microseconds. Seeking on a spinning hard disk takes about 10 milliseconds. A trip between two far-apart data centers takes about 150 milliseconds.

To feel how big these gaps are, imagine slowing time down so that 1 nanosecond becomes 1 second. Then a CPU cache read takes half a second, a memory read takes about 100 seconds (under two minutes), an SSD read takes about 1.7 days, a round trip inside a data center takes about 5.8 days, a hard disk seek takes about 115 days, and a trip between regions takes nearly five years. It shows why one careless disk read in a hot path can ruin performance.

Three lessons come from these numbers. Memory is fast and disk is slow, which is why we put hot data in a cache. Avoid disk seeks when possible, and prefer sequential reads and writes because they are much faster than random ones. Compress data before sending it over the network, because the network is slow compared with the CPU, so spending a little CPU to send fewer bytes usually wins.

Another practical lesson is about distance: calls inside one data center take around half a millisecond, but calls between regions take hundreds of times longer. A design that makes many cross-region calls in a row will feel slow however fast each server is. These numbers are rough (the table is from around 2020) and hardware keeps improving, but the relative sizes stay about the same, and that is what you need in an interview.

Interview questions and answers

Which is faster, memory or SSD, and by how much?
Memory (about 100 ns) is roughly 1,500 times faster than an SSD random read (about 150 µs). That is why hot data is kept in a memory cache.
Why should we compress data before sending it over the network?
Because network transfer is slow compared with CPU work. A little CPU time to compress usually saves far more time in transfer.
Cross question: Why is sequential disk access faster than random?
Random access makes a hard disk move its head for every read (about 10 ms each), while sequential access reads data one after another with no extra movement. Many databases use append-only logs for this reason.
What does the 150 ms inter-region number teach us?
Avoid chatty calls across regions. Keep related services close, replicate data to where users are, and batch cross-region requests.
Cross question: How would you use these numbers to justify a cache?
A database read from disk might take about 10 ms, while a cache hit takes around 1 ms including the network. For a hot item read thousands of times, that saving, and the reduced load on the database, easily justifies the cache.

🟢Step 3 Availability numbers (the nines)

Hinglish samajh: Availability batati hai ki system kitne samay up rehta hai. 99% ko do nines, 99.9% ko teen nines kehte hain. Saal mein 99% par lagbhag 3.65 din, 99.9% par 8.8 ghante, 99.99% par 52 minute, 99.999% par 5.3 minute aur 99.9999% par sirf 31 second downtime milta hai. Har extra nine par downtime das guna kam hota hai par kharcha bahut badhta hai. Series mein components ki availability guna hoti hai (kam ho jati hai), aur parallel mein (backup ke saath) badh jati hai. Cloud SLA aam taur par 99.9% ya usse zyada hota hai.

Availability tells you what part of the time a system is up and working. It is written as a percentage, and people describe it by counting the "nines": 99% is "two nines", 99.9% is "three nines", and so on. Each extra nine looks like a tiny change on paper, but it makes a very large difference in how much downtime you are allowed.

Here is what each level means over one year. At 99% (two nines) you may be down about 3.65 days. At 99.9% (three nines) it is about 8.8 hours. At 99.99% (four nines) it is about 52 minutes. At 99.999% (five nines) it is about 5.3 minutes. At 99.9999% (six nines) it is only about 31.6 seconds. Every extra nine cuts the allowed downtime to one tenth, but it also makes the system much harder and more expensive to build.

Cloud providers such as Amazon, Google and Microsoft commonly promise 99.9% or higher in their SLAs (Service Level Agreements). It helps to know three related words. An SLI (indicator) is something you measure, like the percentage of successful requests. An SLO (objective) is the target you set for it, like 99.9%. An SLA is the agreement with a customer, often with money back if the target is missed.

Availability also combines in simple ways. If a request must pass through components in series (one after the other), the total availability is the multiplication of each one, so it is lower than any single part. Two 99.9% parts in series give about 99.8%. If you place components in parallel with a backup (either one can serve), the system fails only if both fail, which makes it much more available: two 99.9% servers in parallel give 99.9999%. This is the mathematical reason for redundancy.

  • Each extra nine is much more costly than the last.
  • Parallel math assumes independent failures, which is not always true (shared power, same bug).
  • Counting only server uptime can hide partial failures such as slow responses.

Interview questions and answers

What does "three nines" mean?
99.9% availability, which allows about 8.8 hours of downtime per year (about 43 minutes per month).
Why is each extra nine so hard?
It reduces allowed downtime by 10 times, so you need more redundancy, better monitoring, automatic failover and careful releases, and every part must meet the target.
Cross question: A request goes through three services of 99.9% each. What is the total?
About 0.999 x 0.999 x 0.999 = 0.997, so 99.7%. More dependencies in a chain lower the total.
What is the difference between SLI, SLO and SLA?
SLI is the measurement (for example success rate), SLO is the internal target for it (for example 99.9%), and SLA is the contract with the customer, usually with penalties.
Cross question: Does two servers in parallel really give 99.9999%?
Only on paper. It assumes the two fail independently and failover is perfect. In real systems shared parts (a load balancer, a database, a deployment) lower the real number, so be careful.

🐦Step 4 Worked example: Twitter QPS and storage

Hinglish samajh: Twitter wala example: 300 million MAU, 50% roz use karte hain, yaani 150 million DAU. Har user 2 tweets karta hai, to roz 300 million tweets. Ek din mein 86,400 second hote hain, to average QPS lagbhag 3,500, aur peak ke liye double karke 7,000. Storage: sirf 10% tweets mein media (1 MB), yaani roz 30 TB, aur 5 saal mein lagbhag 55 PB. Text ka storage bahut chhota hai (roz lagbhag 60 GB). Isse samajh aata hai ki asli kharcha media par hai. Hamesha pehle assumptions likhein aur phir step by step calculate karein.

Now let us use everything in one realistic example. Suppose the interviewer says: "Estimate the traffic and storage for a Twitter-like service." We never start calculating immediately. First we write down the assumptions, then we calculate step by step.

The assumptions are these. The service has 300 million monthly active users. 50% of them use it every day. Each user posts 2 tweets per day on average. 10% of tweets contain media (an image or video). Data is kept for 5 years. Notice that each assumption is short and has a clear unit.

First we find the traffic. Daily active users are 300 million x 50% = 150 million. Tweets per day are 150 million x 2 = 300 million. A day has 24 x 3600 = 86,400 seconds, so the average is 300 million / 86,400, which is about 3,500 tweets per second (QPS, queries per second). Traffic is not flat during the day, so we assume the peak is twice the average: about 7,000 QPS. This peak number is what we must design for.

Next we find storage. We assume a tweet has a 64-byte id, 140 bytes of text, and, for tweets with media, 1 MB of media. Only 10% of tweets have media, so the media per day is 150 million users x 2 tweets x 10% x 1 MB = 30 TB per day. Over 5 years that is 30 TB x 365 x 5, which is about 55 PB (petabytes). This number tells us we need a distributed object store and cannot keep media in one database.

A good habit is to compare the parts. Text storage is tiny in comparison: 300 million tweets x about 200 bytes is about 60 GB per day, which is around 110 TB in five years. So media is about 500 times bigger than text, and that is where the real cost lives. This kind of insight is exactly what impresses an interviewer. (The text and bandwidth figures here are my own extra calculations; the base numbers come from the original notes.)

How it works, step by step

  1. Write down the assumptions (MAU, DAU share, tweets per user, media share, retention).
  2. Convert monthly users to daily users, then to tweets per day.
  3. Divide by 86,400 seconds to get average QPS, then double it for peak QPS.
  4. Multiply media tweets by the media size to get storage per day.
  5. Multiply by 365 and by the number of years to get total storage.
  6. Compare the parts (media vs text) and say what the numbers mean for the design.

Interview questions and answers

Walk me through estimating Twitter tweet QPS.
Assume 300M monthly users and 50% daily use, so 150M DAU. At 2 tweets each that is 300M tweets per day. Divide by 86,400 seconds for about 3,500 QPS, and assume peak is twice that, about 7,000 QPS.
How did you get 55 PB of storage?
Only 10% of the 300M daily tweets carry 1 MB of media: 30M x 1 MB = 30 TB per day. Multiply by 365 days and 5 years to get about 55 PB.
Cross question: What did you forget?
Replication (for example 3 copies triples the storage), thumbnails and multiple video sizes, indexes, and read traffic such as timelines. A strong answer mentions these as the next refinements.
Cross question: How would you estimate the number of servers?
Assume what one server can handle, for example 1,000 requests per second (an assumption that must be confirmed). Then 7,000 peak QPS / 1,000 = 7 servers, and add spare capacity for failures and deployments, so perhaps 10.
How would the design change because of the 55 PB number?
No single database can hold it. Store media in a distributed object store with a CDN for delivery, keep metadata (tweet id, text, user) in a sharded database, and move old media to cheaper storage.

💡Step 5 Tips for effective estimation

Hinglish samajh: Estimation ek aadat hai. Pehli aadat: numbers ko round karo (99,987 / 9.1 ko 100,000 / 10 = 10,000 maan lo). Doosri: assumptions likho. Teesri: units zaroor likho (5 MB, sirf 5 nahi). Chauthi: common cheezein practice karo: QPS, peak QPS, storage, cache memory aur servers ki sankhya. Yaad rakhein: 1 million requests/din lagbhag 12 QPS hota hai, aur peak ke liye average ko kam se kam double karein. Replication ke copies bhi storage mein jodna na bhoolein.

Good estimation is a habit more than a talent. The first habit is rounding and approximation. Precision is not important; the process is. Replace awkward numbers with friendly ones. For example, 99,987 divided by 9.1 is hard to do in your head, but if you turn it into 100,000 divided by 10, the answer is 10,000, and that is close enough. Use powers of ten whenever you can.

The second habit is to write down your assumptions. Put them at the top of the board, so you and the interviewer can see them, challenge them and change them. When an assumption changes, you recalculate from that one line instead of starting over.

The third habit is to label units. Write "5 MB" instead of just "5". A missing unit is the easiest way to be off by a factor of a thousand, and it is also the easiest mistake to avoid. Also keep track of the time unit: is that number per second, per day or per month?

The fourth habit is to know the common estimation scenarios. QPS measures traffic intensity. Peak QPS accounts for spikes, usually a multiple of the average. Storage is the total data you must keep. Cache is the memory needed for hot data (a common rule of thumb is that a small share of data, around 20%, serves most reads, so you might cache about that much of the daily data). Servers are the machines needed to handle peak QPS. Practice with these five until each one feels routine.

  • QPS (Queries Per Second): Measure traffic intensity.
  • Peak QPS: Account for traffic spikes.
  • Storage Requirements: Estimate total data needs.
  • Cache Requirements: Evaluate memory requirements for caching.

Interview questions and answers

What are your top tips for estimation?
Round numbers, write down assumptions, label units, and practice the standard scenarios: QPS, peak QPS, storage, cache and servers.
Convert 1 million requests per day into QPS.
About 1,000,000 / 86,400, which is about 12 requests per second. A billion per day is about 12,000 QPS.
Cross question: Why use peak QPS instead of average?
Because the system must survive the busiest moments. If you size for the average, it fails during a spike. Use at least 2 times the average, more for events like sales.
Practice: 1 million photo uploads per day at 2 MB each, kept for a year. Storage?
1M x 2 MB = 2 TB per day. 2 TB x 365 = 730 TB, about 0.7 PB. With 3 copies it is about 2.2 PB.
Practice: how big a cache for 100 GB of daily read data?
Using the rule of thumb that about 20% of data serves most reads, cache around 20 GB. It is an assumption you should state.

🎯Step 6 Putting it together in an interview

Hinglish samajh: Interview mein estimation design discussion ke beech mein aata hai aur sirf kuch minute leta hai. Routine yaad rakhein: pehle scale clarify karo, phir assumptions likho aur confirm karao, phir round numbers se calculate karo, phir sanity check karo, aur aakhir mein batao ki in numbers ka design par kya asar hai. Jaise 7,000 peak QPS ka matlab load balancer aur kai servers, 55 PB ka matlab distributed storage aur sharding, aur zyada reads ka matlab cache aur replicas. Galat assumption chalega agar aap use saaf bolte ho, par chhupi hui galti nahi.

In a real interview, estimation is rarely a separate exam. It appears in the middle of a design discussion, usually right after you clarify the requirements. The calculation should take only a few minutes, and its goal is to guide the design: how many servers, which database, whether to shard, whether a cache is needed, and how big the network must be.

A simple routine works well. First, clarify the scale: how many users, how many are active, and what do they do? Second, state your assumptions and confirm them. Third, calculate with round numbers and labelled units. Fourth, sanity check: does the result seem believable compared with known systems or your latency numbers? Fifth, explain what it means for the design. The last step is the one many candidates forget, and it is the one that turns arithmetic into architecture.

For example, if peak traffic is 7,000 QPS and one server handles about 1,000 QPS (an assumption), you need around seven servers plus spare ones, so a load balancer and several stateless servers make sense. If storage reaches 55 PB, a single database is impossible and you need distributed storage. If the cache hit rate must be high and memory is 100 times faster than SSD, a cache is justified. Numbers lead to decisions.

Finally, remember the attitude. Be confident but flexible, show your work, and invite correction. A wrong assumption that you state clearly is fine, because you can fix it in seconds. A silent mistake is not.

How it works, step by step

  1. Clarify the scale and the main use cases.
  2. State and confirm the assumptions.
  3. Calculate with round numbers and labelled units.
  4. Sanity check the result against what you know.
  5. Explain what the numbers mean for the design.

✨ Features

  • A short, repeatable routine.
  • Numbers connect directly to design decisions.
  • Assumptions can be changed at any time.

Interview questions and answers

How long should estimation take in an interview?
A few minutes. Do just enough to guide the design, then move on.
Cross question: How do you sanity check an estimate?
Compare with something you know (for example a similar famous service), re-check the units and the time unit, and see if the result is within a believable order of magnitude.
What do you do if you do not remember a number, such as a server's QPS?
State a reasonable assumption out loud, mark it as an assumption, and ask if it is acceptable. Interviewers care about reasoning, not memory.
How do estimates drive the design?
Peak QPS decides the number of servers, storage decides the database and sharding choice, read and write ratios decide caching and replicas, and bandwidth decides the use of a CDN.
